Founder & CFP® Professional

Meet Austin Mall, Financial Philosopher

Dear Reader,

I'm Austin Mall, educator, former seminarian, husband, and father.

During work hours, I help families navigate complex financial decisions. Here, I have the opportunity to reflect more deeply on the ideas that surround money, happiness, purpose, and the values we cherish most.

I want more people to be wise with their money, time, and talents so they can devote more of themselves to what is truly worth caring about. That's easier said than done. Money can open doors, solve problems, and create opportunities. It can also distract us from the very things we hope it will help us achieve.

That's why I created FinancialPhilosopher.net.

Many people are skeptical of financial professionals, and often for good reason. Some have been disappointed. Others simply don't know whom to trust. My hope is that this site can serve as a different kind of resource, one focused on thoughtful reflection, practical wisdom, and honest conversations about money and life. Together, these topics help us explore how money works and what money is for.

I built this site because I believe the philosophy behind my planning practice can help individuals, families, and organizations discover what real wealth actually requires. This community is about ensuring that our financial lives remain at the service of our deeper callings.

Look around. Despite unprecedented material abundance, many people remain restless, anxious, and dissatisfied. It is tempting to believe that the next purchase, achievement, or financial milestone will finally be enough. Yet most of us eventually discover that money is a valuable tool, but an undeserving master.

My hope is that FinancialPhilosopher.net becomes a place of genuine encouragement, reflection, and growth. A place worth revisiting. A place worth sharing with family and friends.

Welcome, and thank you for being here.

Austin MallAustin Mall, CFP®

Give Caesar His Due, and Not a Moment More

      
By Austin Mall, CFP® Professional at FinancialPhilosopher.net

    

    

      
A couple weeks ago, we were preparing to go to a birthday party for my son's friend. Keep in mind that my son is five years old. Half an hour before we were set to leave, my son asked me, with a worried look, "Dad, I don't know what to give my friend for his birthday." Not having enough time to run to the store, I advised him to look around and see if he could make something or consider giving one of his own toys (a typical Dad response; Mom would have been more helpful). Like any five-year-old, he began deliberating which of his toys he could part with and which ones he wanted to keep for himself.

      
We can all relate. We all have gifts (the Church often calls them time, talent, and treasure), and we must decide how willing or unwilling we are to part with them. The Bible provides a practical method for prioritizing our gifts. The Pharisees and Herodians try to set a trap for Jesus by asking him if it is lawful to pay taxes to the Emperor (Mt 22:15-22 NABRE). By "lawful," they mean according to God's law. Jesus, knowing their deception, said, "Show me the coin that pays the census tax." Then they handed him the Roman coin. He asked them, "Whose image is this and whose inscription?" They replied, "Caesar's." At that he [Jesus] said to them, "Then repay to Caesar what belongs to Caesar and to God what belongs to God."

      
Understanding Jesus's wise response – relating it to our everyday tasks and more significant life projects – can help us make room for doing more of the things that matter most.

      
Jesus uses money, a worldly tool familiar to everyone, to showcase our true identity. Jesus points out that the coin belongs to Caesar because it bears his image. Correspondingly, whatever bears God's image belongs to God. The question Jesus doesn't immediately answer – but leaves open for the audience to consider – is: What, then, is that object that bears God's image? For the Jewish audience of Jesus's time and for us today, Sacred Scripture teaches that God's image is written on humanity. This is also known as the Imago Dei: Man and woman are made in the image and likeness of God. Here's how I hear Jesus's reply: Why are you asking me about taxes? Pay them. This money is not really yours because you will not keep it forever. Care more about the one whose image is written on your innermost being, namely God, and give to Him all those things that belong to Him, namely your entire being because this is what you will keep for all eternity. That's quite an invitation and one we may want to jump ahead to, but let's not overlook the importance of what Jesus said first about paying taxes.

      
In life, especially finances, there are things you must do, and typically, the better you are at mundane things the more capacity you have for taking on the more important, often complex, life projects. For families, there are boxes that, though not exciting, if left unchecked can lead to unnecessary risks and missing out on long-term goals entirely. Financially, these tasks include managing cash flow, applying for relevant insurance policies, getting a will and other estate planning documents in order, saving for retirement, and so on. All these items, like paying taxes, aren't necessarily bad things; in fact, getting them completed frees you to look beyond this week's tasks. Or better, you can automate those tasks. Some examples include setting up recurring contributions to savings accounts and calendar reminders to review your cash flow, estate planning documents, and insurance policies.

      
What can you focus on when you're not drowning in busy work? Here are some concrete examples of projects that my wife and I are working on, which I believe fit the category of "giving God what belongs to God": raising our children well, giving back to our Church and community, incorporating the liturgical calendar into our daily living, spending time with other families, and working hard in our vocations.

      
I want to finish the birthday story. My son decided to give his friend one of his most cherished dump trucks, and instead of feeling the loss, he was thrilled to see his friend smile at receiving the gift. Adults, including myself, can learn from this. He gave his best to someone who bears God's image, and that's exactly where our best belongs.

      
In my annual calendar, I have a list of life projects that mean the most to me (for example: my faith, wife, children, work, health, and leisure). Next to each project, I have a vision and several recurring tasks. Next to "Faith," I have written "to become a saint" (the vision), followed by "regular confession and prayer" (the tasks). I'd encourage you to do the same with your most important life projects. If there are goals you can't imagine seeing, congratulations, you've made a good list.

    

    
Austin MallAustin Mall, CFP®
